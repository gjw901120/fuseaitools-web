            <h2 style="color:#f9fafb;border-bottom-color:#374151;">The Upgrade Ladder: Three Legal Rungs, Priced as Insurance</h2>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;">The <a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/grok/upscale" style="color:#fb7185;text-decoration:none;">upscale lane</a> is where the family's design opinions show most clearly. It bills per second <em>of the source video's actual duration</em>, across exactly three legal combinations: <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">480p→720p at 15/s, 720p→1080p at 30/s, 480p→1080p at 45/s</strong>. Anything else — including any attempt to upscale a 1080p source — finds no pricing row and is refused. Two facts fall out of those three numbers. First, the ladder is perfectly additive: 15 + 30 = 45, so taking the stairs through a 720p intermediate costs <em>exactly</em> what the single jump does — you are never double-taxed for stopping on the landing, which also means the intermediate step is never wasted money if you wanted the 720p file too. Second, and this is the insurance part: every upgrade rung costs <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">more per second than just rendering at the target</strong> — 15 against 12 for 720p, 30 and 45 against 24 for 1080p. Upscaling on this card is not a cost optimization. It is what you buy when the composition is already approved and re-rendering it at higher resolution risks getting a <em>different video</em>.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;">Run the canonical example: you need 8 seconds at 1080p. Rendering native on the mainline bills 8 × 24 = <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">192</strong>. Rendering it at 480p (48) and upgrading bills 8 × 45 = 360 on top, <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">408 total — more than double</strong>. Nobody takes that route for the savings. They take it when the 480p file already won the room and the client wants it big.</p>

The 8-second example is the proof: native 1080p bills 192; rendering 480p and upgrading bills 408 — more than double. Upscale buys composition lock-in, not savings, and quoting it as a cost play overprices the exact thing it exists to protect.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Multiplying extend like a per-second lane.</strong> Extend is the family's flat-menu fossil: 6 or 10 seconds only, 480p or 720p only, prices 24/45/42/72 per order. A "12-second 480p extension at 4.2/s" is not a quote — the lane will not sell 12 seconds, and its effective rates come from dividing rows the menu never divides for you.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Ordering 1080p on the preview lane.</strong> The upstream model can do it; the card cannot. 1080p has no pricing row on video-1-5-preview and the request is refused before a job is created. Final-resolution work belongs on the mainline, which is the whole point of the tier split.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Assuming the two duration windows are interchangeable.</strong> The mainline starts at 6 seconds; the preview stops at 15. A 3-second sting cannot be bought on the mainline at any price, and a 20-second cut cannot be bought on the preview — the overlap is only 6 through 15, and outside it the lane choice is made for you.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Trying to upgrade from the top rung.</strong> Upscale has exactly three legal source→target combinations and 1080p appears in none of the source positions: a finished 1080p clip has nowhere to go up to, and a 720p source can only go to 1080p, not sideways. Design the ladder walk before you generate, because the card will not let you improvise it.</p>
