            <h2 style="color:#f9fafb;border-bottom-color:#374151;">Act I: The Flat Menu — and the Discount Hidden in Its Last Row</h2>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/wan/text-to-video" style="color:#38bdf8;text-decoration:none;">Wan 2.6 text-to-video</a> (and its <a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/wan/image-to-video" style="color:#38bdf8;text-decoration:none;">image-to-video</a> and <a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/wan/video-to-video" style="color:#38bdf8;text-decoration:none;">video-to-video</a> twins) priced by absolute per-clip menus on a duration × quality grid. At 720p the menu was honest to the point of boring: 5 seconds = <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">120</strong>, 10 = <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">240</strong>, 15 = <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">360</strong> — exactly 24 credits a second at every row. Do the division on the 1080p column and the shape changes: 180, 360, and then <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">480 for 15 seconds</strong>. Five seconds and ten divide to 36/s; the long row divides to <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">32/s</strong>. Nobody announced it, but the menu was doing what airline pricing does — rewarding the commitment to a longer render with a lower unit rate, one tier down, where the arithmetic only shows up if you go looking.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;">Why does a hidden unit-rate discount matter? Because it is invisible to exactly the two decisions it should inform: choosing duration ("15 seconds is 480, fine") and choosing generation ("the 36-a-second 1080p lane"). It is visible only in the division nobody performs. Menus price packages; they do not price seconds — and every downstream generation in this story is a reaction to that gap.</p>

            <h2 style="color:#f9fafb;border-bottom-color:#374151;">Act II: The Flat-Match — 2.7 Converted the Rate, and Deleted the Fifth Row</h2>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/wan/v2-7-text-to-video" style="color:#38bdf8;text-decoration:none;">Wan 2.7 text-to-video</a> flipped the billing mode: once.credits now stores a <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">per-second rate</strong> (duration=1 rule × actual seconds), and the menu rows — all of them, on all four 2.7 lanes including <a href="https://www.fuseaitools.com/home/wan/v2-7-r2v" style="color:#38bdf8;text-decoration:none;">r2v</a> — collapse into two numbers: <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">24/s at 720p, 36/s at 1080p</strong>. Read those against Act I and the conversion strategy is explicit: the per-second rates are the old menu's <em>short-row</em> unit rates, kept digit for digit. Nothing about a 5- or 10-second render changed by a single credit; 2.7's whole innovation was legibility. You could finally quote a Wan render by multiplying, and the price of "one second more" became a constant.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;">But constants cut both ways, and the 15-second 1080p row is where this one lands: at 36/s, fifteen seconds bill <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">540</strong> — sixty credits more than the old menu's 480. The discount didn't expire; <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">it couldn't survive the unit change</strong>. A flat menu can embed any curve it likes between rows; a per-second rate is, by definition, a straight line. Transparency and concession were the same lever pulled one way, and teams repricing their long-form renders from muscle memory got the short end of the trade — a 12.5% tax that only exists if you were previously collecting an unadvertised subsidy that only existed if you divided.</p>

The 720p rate is now three generations unchanged — 24 credits a second is the steadiest number in Wan's history, the lane where flat-menu and per-second pricing agreed to stop arguing. The movement is at the edges: a new <strong style="color:#f9fafb;">480p floor at half the 720p rate</strong> (a 5-second draft costs 60 — the same number 2.6 charged for 720p, two rungs lower), and the 1080p ceiling lifted from 36 to 48. Read together: the duration discount of the menu era is gone for good, and the quality premium now does all the pricing work. Duration stopped being a deal and became a meter; resolution is where the negotiation happens.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;">Two design decisions complete the generation.             <h2 style="color:#f9fafb;border-bottom-color:#374151;">Pitfalls: Five Ways Old Wan Habits Misprice New Wan</h2>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Carrying the long-render discount forward.</strong> 15 seconds of 1080p at 480 was a menu-era number. It became 540 the day per-second billing arrived, and 720 at 3.0 — sheets that still assume "long rows are cheaper per second" overpay invisibly in the other direction, by up to half.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Buying prime for quality.</strong> 3.0 prime bills exactly 2× standard (20/40/80) on an identical input contract — the same resolutions, the same durations. That doubling buys speed, not pixels; paying it to get "more 1080p" is the most expensive category error on this family's card.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Planning an audio surcharge.</strong> Wan 3.0's rate is audio-indifferent — the switch does not move the per-second price. Teams porting budget logic from sound-priced families (Kling's +10/s at 720p) allocate a premium that does not exist here, then wonder why the invoice reads light.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Quoting the 480p floor at 720p rates.</strong> The 12/s lane is new with 3.0; nothing below 720p existed in the 2.x menus. Drafts quoted at 24/s against a 3.0 render are budgeted double — and the 5-second, 60-credit draft is the number most legacy sheets never learned to spend.</p>
            <p style="color:#d1d5db;"><strong style="color:#f9fafb;">Leaving duration to "smart."</strong> 3.0 rejects the vendor's -1 auto-duration; seconds are a manual 2–30 choice and every one of them bills. The menu era's implicit rounding (you could only pick 5, 10, 15) has been replaced by an obligation to decide — a 17-second cut bills 17, not "the 15 row."</p>
